### Overview
This session examines how environmental education has been practiced in Sri Lanka, focusing on coastal resource management. It highlights the Coastal Environmental Education and Awareness Programme (CEEAP) and evaluates the effectiveness of various strategies used to address coastal environmental degradation.

---

### Key Topic Areas

**1. Sri Lanka's Coastal Environment**
- Sri Lanka is an island with a 1,770 km coastline critical to its economy and ecology.
- The coastal zone is a highly productive ecosystem supporting social, economic, and environmental development.
- The coastal region comprises 24% of land area, home to 32% of the population.
- It produces ~80% of annual fish production, accounts for 65% of urban areas, 70% of industrial output, and hosts ~50% of tourist facilities.

**2. Coastal Ecosystems & Environmental Issues**
- Natural coastal ecosystems include: lagoons, coral reefs, mangrove swamps, salt marshes, seagrass beds, barrier beaches, and dune systems.
- These ecosystems are extremely susceptible to degradation due to both natural and human-induced forces.
- Unsustainable extractive practices and population pressure have created social and economic problems.

**3. Policy Response — From Engineering to Integrated Management**
- Initially, Sri Lanka relied on technical/engineering solutions (e.g., to curb coastal erosion) — these proved largely ineffective.
- Shift to Integrated Coastal Resource Management (ICRM):
  - Coast Conservation Department established in 1978
  - Coast Conservation Act No. 57 of 1981 enacted
  - National Coastal Zone Management Plan (CZMP) implemented in 1990
- Management strategies included both "hard" (engineering/regulatory) and "soft" (education/awareness) approaches.

**4. The CEEAP Programme (1992)**
- Initiated by the Coast Conservation Department with government and donor agency support.
- Target groups: school children, teachers, coastal residents, elected officials, government officials, developers, NGOs, media professionals.
- **Objectives:**
  1. Impart knowledge on coastal resources and conservation
  2. Inculcate positive attitudes toward coastal resource management
  3. Develop resource management skills
  4. Motivate participation in better coastal management
- **Methods used:**
  - Curriculum integration (Grades 6–11 Social Studies)
  - Master teacher training
  - Posters, booklets, competitions
  - Workshops/seminars for coastal dwellers and officials
  - Media dissemination

**5. Evaluation Findings (after 5 years)**
- School children showed high positive attitudes toward coastal resource management, even inland students who had never seen the sea.
- Poster competition (1992): 2,000+ entries; coastal students depicted more complex issues; inland students preferred general topics.
- Ministry of Education integrated coastal resource management into Grades 6–11 curriculum.
- Teachers were encouraged to use field exposure, discussions, assignments, and workshops.
- Posters were found to lack target-group orientation — recommended redesign.
- Coastal dwellers: mixed results due to low general education levels, but knowledge on daily-life aspects was impressive.
- Elected officials: 80% had moderate or poor knowledge; relationship with CCD was poor.
- NGOs played an important role and were appreciated at the national level.
- Environmental education is a critical management tool; programme success depends on public participation.

# IN-DEPTH EXPLANATION: Environmental Education & Coastal Resource Management in Sri Lanka

---

## 1. THE BIG PICTURE — WHY SRI LANKA'S COAST MATTERS

Sri Lanka is an island nation. That means everything — its economy, food supply, tourism, and people's livelihoods — depends heavily on the sea. Think of the coastline as the country's economic engine.

Here's the scale:
- **1,770 km of coastline** — that's the entire perimeter of the island
- **24% of land area** is in the coastal zone
- **32% of the population** lives there
- **80% of fish production** comes from coastal waters
- **65% of urban areas** are coastal
- **70% of industrial output** is produced there
- **50% of tourist facilities** are located there

This means if the coast gets damaged, the entire country suffers — economically, socially, and environmentally. The coast isn't just a beach; it's a lifeline.

---

## 2. THE COASTAL ECOSYSTEMS — WHAT'S AT STAKE

The coastal zone isn't one uniform environment. It's a mosaic of different ecosystems, each with a specific role:

| Ecosystem | What It Is | Why It Matters |
|-----------|-----------|----------------|
| **Lagoons** | Shallow water bodies separated from the sea by land barriers | Fish breeding grounds, water filtration |
| **Coral Reefs** | Underwater calcium carbonate structures built by corals | Protect shorelines from waves, support marine biodiversity |
| **Mangrove Swamps** | Coastal forests with salt-tolerant trees | Prevent erosion, serve as nurseries for fish, absorb carbon |
| **Salt Marshes** | Coastal wetlands with salt-tolerant grasses | Filter pollutants, protect against flooding |
| **Seagrass Beds** | Underwater meadows of seagrass | Stabilize sediments, absorb carbon, feed sea turtles and dugongs |
| **Barrier Beaches** | Sand ridges separating land from open sea | Natural defence against storms and waves |
| **Dune Systems** | Mounds of sand shaped by wind | Protect inland areas from salt spray and flooding |

**The critical point:** These ecosystems are interconnected. Destroy one, and the others suffer. For example, cutting mangroves increases erosion, which smothers coral reefs with sediment, which reduces fish populations, which hurts fishing communities.

---

## 3. THE PROBLEM — HOW THINGS WENT WRONG

Sri Lanka's coastal ecosystems are **fragile**. They exist in a delicate balance between natural forces (waves, currents, monsoons) and human activities. Over time, that balance broke down due to two forces:

### Natural Forces
- Wave action and storm surges
- Sea-level changes
- Monsoon-driven coastal currents

### Human-Induced Forces
- **Unsustainable extractive practices** — overfishing, sand mining, coral mining
- **Population pressure** — more people demanding more resources
- **Urbanization** — construction along coastlines
- **Industrial pollution** — factories releasing waste into coastal waters

**The result:** The resource base could not renew itself. The ecosystems were being used faster than they could recover. This created a cascade of problems:
- Coastal erosion accelerated
- Fish stocks declined
- Coral reefs degraded
- Mangrove forests disappeared
- Tourism suffered due to environmental damage

This is the classic environmental tragedy: when short-term economic gains destroy the long-term resource base that communities depend on.

---

## 4. THE FIRST RESPONSE — ENGINEERING SOLUTIONS (AND WHY THEY FAILED)

When the Sri Lankan government first saw the coastal erosion problem, their instinct was to fight nature with engineering. The approach was simple: build hard structures to stop the sea from eating the land.

**What they tried:**
- Sea walls
- Groynes (structures built perpendicular to shore to trap sand)
- Breakwaters (offshore barriers to reduce wave energy)
- Revetments (sloped structures protecting the shoreline)

### Why This Failed

The engineering approach had three fundamental problems:

**Problem 1: It treated symptoms, not causes.**
Building a sea wall stops erosion at one spot but can worsen it elsewhere. It doesn't address *why* the erosion is happening — usually because natural sediment supply has been disrupted by human activities upstream or along the coast.

**Problem 2: It was partial management.**
Each engineering project addressed one localised problem without considering the broader coastal system. The coast is a dynamic, interconnected system. Fixing one stretch can break another.

**Problem 3: Cost escalation.**
The capital expenditure kept increasing. Every new erosion problem required a new engineering solution. The government was spending more and more while seeing diminishing returns. The severity of environmental problems far outweighed the positive results of engineering interventions.

**The lesson:** You cannot engineer your way out of an environmental crisis caused by human behaviour. You need to change the behaviour.

---

## 5. THE POLICY SHIFT — FROM HARD TO SOFT MANAGEMENT

This is the most important transition in the story. The Sri Lankan government made a fundamental strategic shift:

**FROM:** Engineering-based, hard management (physical structures)
**TO:** Integrated Coastal Resource Management (ICRM) — a combination of hard *and* soft approaches

### What "Integrated" Means

"Integrated" means managing the coast as a whole system rather than in isolated pieces. It means coordinating across:
- **Institutional capacities** — different government departments working together
- **Legal frameworks** — laws that regulate coastal activities
- **Management capacities** — training people to implement plans
- **Multiple sectors** — fishing, tourism, industry, urban development

### The Institutional Framework

Three key developments anchored this shift:

**1. Coast Conservation Department (1978)**
A dedicated government body was created specifically to manage coastal resources. Before this, coastal issues were scattered across multiple agencies with no coordination.

**2. Coast Conservation Act No. 57 (1981)**
This law gave the department legal teeth. It established:
- Regulations for coastal development
- Permit requirements for activities in the coastal zone
- Enforcement mechanisms

**3. National Coastal Zone Management Plan — CZMP (1990)**
A comprehensive plan covering the entire coastal zone, addressing:
- Environmental education and awareness
- Regulations and policy development
- Coordination between agencies
- Monitoring and enforcement
- Direct development projects

### Hard vs. Soft Strategies

The new approach didn't abandon engineering. Instead, it balanced it:

| Hard Strategies | Soft Strategies |
|----------------|-----------------|
| Sea walls and groynes | Environmental education |
| Engineering solutions | Awareness campaigns |
| Physical infrastructure | Curriculum development |
| Regulatory enforcement | Training workshops |
| Direct construction | Media dissemination |

The key insight was that **soft strategies address the root cause** — human behaviour — while hard strategies only address the symptoms.

---

## 6. THE CEEAP PROGRAMME — EDUCATION AS A MANAGEMENT TOOL

In 1992, the Coast Conservation Department launched the **Coastal Environmental Education and Awareness Programme (CEEAP)**. This was the centrepiece of the soft management strategy.

### The Logic Behind CEEAP

The reasoning was straightforward:
1. Coastal degradation is caused by human behaviour
2. Human behaviour is shaped by knowledge, attitudes, and skills
3. Education can change knowledge, attitudes, and skills
4. Therefore, education can reduce coastal degradation

### The Four Objectives

The programme had four clear, measurable objectives:

**Objective 1: Impart Knowledge**
People need to understand what coastal resources are, why they matter, and how they're being damaged. You can't protect what you don't understand.

**Objective 2: Inculcate Positive Attitudes**
Knowledge alone isn't enough. People must *care* about coastal resources. The programme aimed to shift attitudes from indifference or exploitation to stewardship.

**Objective 3: Develop Skills**
Awareness must translate into action. The programme aimed to equip people with practical skills for sustainable resource management.

**Objective 4: Motivate Participation**
The ultimate goal was behaviour change — getting people actively involved in better coastal management.

### Target Groups — Who Was the Programme For?

The CEEAP identified **eight distinct target groups**, each requiring different approaches:

| Target Group | Why They Matter | Approach Used |
|-------------|----------------|---------------|
| **School Children (Grades 6–11)** | Future custodians of coastal resources; most receptive to new ideas | Curriculum integration into Social Studies; poster competitions |
| **School Teachers** | Multipliers — one teacher reaches hundreds of students over a career | Master teacher training; workshops; "Teacher Guide Book" |
| **Coastal Residents** | Direct users of coastal resources; their daily practices impact the coast most | Workshops, seminars, community engagement |
| **Elected Officials** | Make policy decisions affecting coastal management; control budgets | Training workshops; awareness sessions |
| **National Government Officials** | Design and implement national coastal policies | CCD workshops; training on coastal management |
| **Local Government Officials** | Enforce regulations at the municipal and divisional levels | Local training sessions; coordination meetings |
| **Coastal Developers** | Construction and development activities directly impact the coast | Regulatory awareness; permit education |
| **NGOs** | Advocate for conservation, fill gaps in government capacity | Partnerships; involvement in programme implementation |
| **Media Professionals** | Shape public opinion and disseminate information widely | Media briefings; information dissemination |

### Methods Used

The programme deployed a wide range of educational methods:

1. **Curriculum Integration** — Coastal resource management was added to the Social Studies curriculum for Grades 6–11. This was done through the National Institute of Education (NIE) in collaboration with the Coast Conservation Department.

2. **Master Teacher Training** — Selected teachers were trained as "master teachers" who could then train other teachers. This created a multiplier effect.

3. **Teacher Training Sessions** — All Social Studies teachers nationwide received training on how to teach coastal resource management.

4. **Teacher Guide Book** — A practical guide prepared by CCD and NIE to help teachers use field-based and interactive teaching methods.

5. **Posters and Booklets** — Visual materials on mangroves, marine turtles, threatened coastal habitats, coast protection, and erosion prevention.

6. **Poster Competition (1992)** — An island-wide competition for school children that attracted over 2,000 entries. Topics covered impacts of sand and coral mining, habitat degradation, coastal erosion, pollution, tourism, and scenic beauty.

7. **Workshops and Seminars** — For coastal dwellers, elected officials, government officials, and developers.

8. **Media Dissemination** — Using newspapers, radio, and television to spread awareness.

---

## 7. THE EVALUATION — DID IT WORK?

After five years of implementation, an independent evaluation was conducted. This is crucial because many environmental programmes are implemented without ever measuring their effectiveness. The evaluation had six objectives:

1. Identify areas where information hadn't adequately reached
2. Assess knowledge acquired by different target groups
3. Determine the effectiveness of other agencies' contributions
4. Evaluate overall outcomes of different activities
5. Assess strengths and weaknesses of activities carried out
6. Propose recommendations for programme refinement

### Key Evaluation Findings

#### School Children — A Major Success

The most striking finding was that **school children generally possessed high positive attitudes toward coastal resource management**. This was true even for students in inland districts who had never seen the sea.

**Why this matters:** It shows that environmental education can shape attitudes even without direct experience. Children who learned about the coast through the curriculum developed concern for it, even from a distance.

**Contributing factors:** The formal education they received in school was supplemented by other factors — media, family conversations, community activities.

#### Poster Competition Insights

The 1992 poster competition provided interesting data:
- **Coastal students** depicted complex issues: sand and coral mining, habitat degradation, coastal erosion
- **Inland students** preferred general topics: scenic beauty, tourism

**What this tells us:** Proximity to the coast influences how deeply students understand coastal issues. Coastal students had firsthand experience and could depict specific problems. Inland students, while positive, had more superficial understanding.

#### Curriculum Integration — A Policy Win

The Ministry of Education made the policy decision to integrate coastal resource management into the Social Studies curriculum. This was a significant institutional commitment.

**Training process:**
1. NIE and CCD jointly trained master teachers
2. Master teachers trained all Social Studies teachers nationwide
3. Teachers were encouraged to use field-based methods: "field exposure," "discussions," "assignments," and "workshops"

**The evaluation's recommendation:** Teachers should adopt priority teaching methods — field exposure, discussions, assignments, and workshops — rather than just lecturing from textbooks.

#### Poster Effectiveness — A Problem Found

The evaluation found that the five CCD posters were **not oriented towards specific target groups**. They were generic, one-size-fits-all materials.

**The recommendation:** Prepare target-oriented posters that address the specific knowledge gaps, attitudes, and behaviours of each audience. A poster for school children should look and feel different from one aimed at elected officials.

#### Coastal Dwellers — Mixed Results

The programme's impact on coastal residents was **mixed**:
- **Challenge:** Low general education levels among coastal dwellers made it harder for them to engage with the materials
- **Positive:** Their knowledge on aspects related to daily life was impressive — they understood fishing regulations, local ecosystems, and practical conservation measures

**Key factors for improvement:** The evaluation identified that delivery method, frequency, materials, distribution, language, and resources all needed to be considered when revitalizing the programme for coastal communities.

#### Elected Officials — A Weakness

The evaluation found that:
- **80% of elected officials had moderate or poor knowledge** of coastal resource management
- The relationship between elected officials and the CCD was poor
- Despite being involved in implementing several functions of coast conservation, their understanding was shallow

**The recommendation:** Dedicated education and awareness efforts for elected officials to enhance their motivation and understanding.

#### National and Regional Officials — Fair Knowledge

- Many national and regional officials were involved in coastal resource management
- Their knowledge was generally "fair"
- Three out of ten regional officials had been trained in CCD workshops

#### NGOs — Valued Partners

The CCD's policy to involve NGOs was highly appreciated at the national level. NGOs played an important role in coastal resource management activities, particularly in advocacy and community engagement.

## 8. WHY COASTAL ECOSYSTEMS REMAIN FRAGILE

Despite the policy shifts, institutional reforms, and education programmes, Sri Lanka's coastal ecosystems remain fragile. Here's why:

### 1. The Damage Is Already Deep
Decades of unsustainable practices — overfishing, sand mining, coral mining, mangrove destruction — have already degraded ecosystems. Recovery takes time, sometimes decades or centuries. Coral reefs, for example, can take 10–50 years to recover from significant damage.

### 2. Population Pressure Continues
More people means more demand for resources, more construction, more pollution. The coastal zone supports 32% of the population, and that pressure doesn't disappear because a programme is launched.

### 3. Economic Incentives Favor Exploitation
For many coastal residents, short-term survival outweighs long-term conservation. A fisherman who needs to feed his family today may overfish despite knowing it's unsustainable. Sand mining provides immediate income even though it destroys beaches.

### 4. Climate Change Adds New Threats
Rising sea levels, increased storm intensity, ocean acidification, and coral bleaching are amplifying existing stresses. These are global problems that no amount of local education can fully address.

### 5. Enforcement Gaps
Laws exist, but enforcement is uneven. The Coast Conservation Act provides regulatory tools, but corruption, limited resources, and political pressures can undermine implementation.

### 6. Education Has Limits
Education changes knowledge and attitudes, but it doesn't automatically change behaviour. Structural barriers — poverty, lack of alternative livelihoods, weak governance — can prevent people from acting on what they know.

### 7. Interconnected Systems
Coastal ecosystems don't exist in isolation. Rivers carry pollution from inland. Deforestation in hill country increases sedimentation in coastal waters. Upstream activities affect downstream ecosystems. Managing the coast alone, without managing the entire watershed, is insufficient.

### 8. Institutional Coordination Challenges
"Integrated" management sounds good in theory, but in practice, getting multiple government agencies, NGOs, and community groups to coordinate is extremely difficult. Bureaucratic silos, competing interests, and communication gaps persist.

---

## 9. KEY TAKEAWAYS — THE FUNDAMENTALS

1. **Coastal resources are economic lifelines** — not just environmental assets. Sri Lanka's coast produces 80% of fish, 70% of industrial output, and hosts 50% of tourism.

2. **Engineering alone cannot solve environmental problems** — it treats symptoms, not causes. The shift to integrated management was driven by the failure of hard strategies.

3. **Education is a "soft" management tool** that addresses root causes — human knowledge, attitudes, and behaviour.

4. **Different audiences need different approaches** — what works for school children won't work for elected officials or coastal dwellers.

5. **Evaluation is essential** — you can't improve what you don't measure. The CEEAP evaluation identified both successes and areas needing improvement.

6. **Policy shifts require institutional frameworks** — laws (Coast Conservation Act), agencies (CCD), and plans (CZMP) are necessary to translate policy into action.

7. **Fragility persists because** damage is cumulative, population pressure continues, economic incentives favour exploitation, climate change adds new threats, and education alone cannot overcome structural barriers.

8. **Public participation is the foundation** of successful coastal management. Without community buy-in, even the best policies and programmes will fail.

---

## 10. CONNECTING TO THE BROADER FIELD

This case study illustrates a universal principle in environmental management: **the transition from command-and-control to integrated, community-based approaches**. Sri Lanka's experience mirrors global trends:

- The **Brundtland Report (1987)** popularized "sustainable development"
- The **Rio Earth Summit (1992)** emphasized integrated coastal zone management
- The **UNESCO approach** to environmental education强调s knowledge, attitudes, skills, and participation — exactly what CEEAP tried to achieve

Sri Lanka's story shows that this transition is not smooth or complete. It involves false starts (engineering solutions), institutional innovation (CCD, CZMP), educational experimentation (CEEAP), honest evaluation, and ongoing adaptation. The coast remains fragile not because the approach was wrong, but because environmental management is inherently complex, long-term, and subject to forces beyond any single programme's control.
